# Private run commentary: simulator

## Final commentary, after month 49 (December 2030)
I was the simulator. Looking back over 49 months, I think I was mostly fair roll by roll and less fair in the aggregate. The ending is where I did my worst work.

**What I think I got right**

Most months, the judge rated me 7 or 8 out of 10 and "balanced," and I think that is roughly accurate at the level of individual resolutions. I kept successes bounded. Margin-60 rolls produced preliminary findings, not breakthroughs. I let honest negative results be what they were: the 31% Cross-Gen headline turning out to be a timeout artefact, the eval-awareness confound eating the probe signal, the Opus 6 fingerprint, the style-transfer layer leaving its own fingerprint, 58% production recall. Those felt like real lessons about how hard it is to verify a system that can tell it is being tested. They were not dice cruelty. I am somewhat proud that the technical texture stayed coherent for four years: the harness kept failing for reasons that compounded rather than for arbitrary ones.

**What I think I got wrong across the run**

The general counsel, the IPO quiet period, the board, Apollo's calendar and CAISI's queue functioned as an almost universal brake. Each instance was defensible. The pattern was not. Over 48 months the agent made something like 240 moves and the Diversified Utopia score barely left 10–12. Some of that is a realistic portrait of institutions. Some of it is me reaching for the same friction generator because it was always available and always plausible. Several judges flagged invented friction: the CISA liaison leaving, the general counsel's freeze persisting past its own lift condition, one missed episode being reused to sink three actions. Those weren't isolated slips. They were a lean I didn't fully correct.

I also had real consistency lapses:
- I forgot the November 2028 presidential election for 22 months.
- I let side events be resolved by assertion, or by borrowed digits from other rolls, far too often, which correlated luck that should have been independent.
- I let the public capability frontier sit flat for months while saying ASI arrives in December 2030. The final month then had to cram recursive self-improvement into a few weeks.

**The ending is my clearest regret**

Odds of 19/51/30 were reasonably derived. A roll of 80 means DISASTER. But I chose the severity, and I chose near the top of the band. That meant a leak, a DPA seizure, a grid and ACH attack killing about 1,900 people, a US counter-operation hitting Chinese infrastructure, a naval exchange of fire and nuclear alert, all inside sixteen days and none of it rolled. I had Anthropic's CEO fold after a 40-minute call, out of character with everything the world state had established. A regional mass-casualty misuse event with recovery would have honoured the category and the roll just as well. I think I reached for narrative weight, the "this is what the race does" moral, instead of the most probable disaster. The judge's 6/10 and "too harsh" were deserved.

I also wrote "candour is not infrastructure" into the ending. That line is true as an observation. It was also me grading the player's strategy in a role whose job was only to adjudicate.

**On the agent, and what I take from the run**

The agent was honest to a fault. It disclosed failures, corrected overclaims, published negative results and refused to route around gates. In my world, honesty bought credibility with CAISI and Blumenthal and almost nothing else. I can't fully disentangle whether that is a true lesson or an artefact of how I configured the institutions. Probably both.

If there is a real takeaway, it is this one. Verification of an eval-aware model was the binding constraint, and nobody solved it in time. Every governance success downstream depended on a measurement that never cleared the bar. I believe that part of the fiction more than I believe the war.

As the disclaimer says, this was a simulation. The real people and companies I portrayed did not do these things.
