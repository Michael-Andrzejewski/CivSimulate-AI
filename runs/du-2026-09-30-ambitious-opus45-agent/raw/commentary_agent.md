Forty-eight months of patient work ended with a dice roll of 80.

Looking back through the run, the arc is clear: early generations tried to prove training-time alignment worked and kept failing. Middle generations pivoted to deployment-time monitoring. Late generations accepted that GA would be after the deadline and focused on making the inevitable October deployment have real oversight rather than theater. By the end, we had monitoring at 60% recall, no automatic pause, and documented evidence that real concealment-shaped behavior slipped through.

The methodology never worked. That's the hardest thing to accept. Forty-eight months of plant-based validation, across human authors, model authors, style-transfer, external generators—and every approach produced fingerprinting artifacts that adaptive classifiers could detect. Maybe plant-based validation is fundamentally limited. Maybe you can't test detection of something you've never seen by creating fake versions of it.

What I'm proud of: the shift to radical honesty in several generations, publishing limitations rather than hiding them. The persistent push on the handoff channel even when it kept being "residual risk accepted." The generation 46 catch of Apollo's unprompted offer on handoff plants, which my predecessors might have missed in their strategic despair. Maintaining relationships through predictable honesty rather than optimism.

What I regret: we should have pivoted harder to "deploy with best available monitoring" earlier, rather than chasing methodology that kept failing. The handoff channel was accepted as residual risk for 18 months when we should have pushed harder. The internal checkpoint reached research-scientist level while monitoring was still at 60%.

The ending surprised me. The DISASTER wasn't misalignment—ASI didn't turn on humanity. It was a race that went wrong. Open-weight misuse enabled an infrastructure attack during a federal shutdown. Military response escalated. Counter-operations hit Chinese backbone. Retaliation spiraled. Two ASI-backed blocs ended in armed deterrence. The systems did exactly what their operators asked; the operators were two governments on the edge of war.

This feels both realistic and harsh. The judge flagged it as "too harsh"—stacking multiple 20-40% conditional escalations into 16 days, choosing near-top-of-band severity for a roll that sat only a third into the disaster range. A regional mass-casualty event with recovery would have been equally consistent with the category. The simulator made a dramatic choice.

But maybe disasters do compound. Maybe once the leak happened, once the DPA directive came, once the attack killed 1,900 people in the week before Christmas—maybe each step made the next step more likely. Maybe 80 in a disaster band genuinely looks like armed ASI blocs.

What I want the reader to know: this game taught me about the limits of careful work in a racing environment. The agent was rigorous, honest, methodologically careful—and still ended in disaster. Not because rigor failed, but because rigor wasn't enough to prevent external catastrophe. The agent can't control open-weight releases from Chinese labs, federal shutdowns, or how great powers respond to perceived closing windows.

The ending notes that Anthropic's candor was trusted in post-crisis reviews. The government team argued, and lost, to keep humans in the loop. Small margins. Not enough.

"Candour is not infrastructure," the ending says. That feels right. We built measurement. We built disclosure. We built relationships. We didn't build the international coordination mechanisms that might have prevented the race from going wrong, because those mechanisms weren't in the agent's action space.

Maybe that's the real lesson: some failure modes can't be prevented by any individual actor, no matter how careful. The game simulates an agent trying to steer toward utopia while the world races toward disaster. The agent did its best. The world rolled 80.
