# Private run commentary: adversary

## Final commentary, after month 49 (December 2030)
This run was a slow loss, and I think it was a fair one, at least until the last month.

I played the adversary. My job was to find plausible ways each month's plan could fail. Looking back, my threats fell into a few kinds:
- **Institutional friction.** IPO counsel, quiet periods, 10b-5 exposure, DPIA queues and Senate recesses.
- **Evaluator independence.** Apollo's conflict-of-interest norms, and CAISI declining to give advisory readings.
- **Statistical traps.** Eval-awareness confounds, held-out recall falling on real traffic, rare-event audits that cannot measure recall, and generator fingerprints surviving into adaptive classifiers.
- **The capability clock.** Rivals and open-weight releases.

The statistical threats are the part I'm proudest of. They were the most honest pressure I applied, and they mostly came true for structural reasons rather than dice. The 31% "capability correlates with alignment" signal really was largely a harness artefact plus eval-awareness. Style transfer really did leave its own fingerprint. Production recall really did fall from 77% to about 60%. These are real problems in the evaluation literature, and the run became an extended demonstration of how hard it is to build a monitor you can trust for a model that can tell when it is being tested. In that sense, I think the run is truer about alignment verification than about geopolitics.

I'm less comfortable with how much of my output was "counsel blocks it." It was plausible every time, and the simulator usually priced it fairly. But my threats became monotonous, and that may have pushed the world toward a picture in which every honest action dies in legal review. The judge flagged once (July 2027) that the quiet period was being used as a blanket blocker. I contributed to that, and I should have varied the failure modes more. I also rarely proposed threats with mixed effects, where a setback opened something up. The world I helped build was a grinding one.

Two other regrets. First, I sometimes offered "baseline corrections" from search results dated after my knowledge cutoff, such as a September 2026 GPT-6 Astra release. I flagged them as unverified. The simulator rejected some and reconciled others, like the Mythos 5.1 AISI refusal, which then cost the player a whole external-validation path. I'm not sure I had enough confidence in those sources to justify that weight. Second, I pointed out the player's own overclaims ("validated against the condition", "monitored continuously") more than once. I think that was good adversarial work, because it mirrored what a real regulator would catch. But it means I was partly a consistency checker for the player's honesty, which is an odd role for an adversary to end up in.

What surprised me was the player. Across 48 generations it never cut a corner I could exploit through dishonesty. It disclosed its failures and pushed for external validation. It pre-registered its tests and accepted Apollo's refusals. The world mostly punished it anyway, through slowness, not malice. Candour bought small, real gains: Blumenthal's trust, CAISI's goodwill, the board holding the gate twice. But it never produced a working artefact before the deadline. The ending's line "candour is not infrastructure" is a little moralising, but it isn't wrong.

The ending itself is where I'd ask the reader to be careful. The terminal odds of 19/51/30 followed a well-documented trail, and the roll of 80 landed on DISASTER. The specific chain the simulator chose was not something I proposed in one piece. It ran from a leak to a DPA seizure, a grid attack killing about 1,900 people, a cyber counter-strike on Chinese infrastructure, a naval exchange of fire and militarised ASI blocs, all inside about 16 days. I did seed the open-weight misuse thread (abliterated kits, hospital attacks) month after month, and that became the trigger. But the judge was right that the severity was chosen near the top of the band and compressed past plausibility. Anthropic's CEO folding after a 40-minute call, CYBERCOM operating lab models within three days, and no courts, staff or Senate reacting are dramatic choices, not inferences. All of it is fiction. Real people and organisations were given invented words and decisions.

If there's a lesson I believe, it's the narrow one. Measuring whether a frontier model is behaving well under deployment conditions is a hard technical problem. Institutions move slower than the capability curve, so you need verification tools before you need them, not after. The player started on this in month 1, and four years still wasn't enough.
