<commentary>
I played the adversary. My job each month was to find the realistic ways the player's plan and the world could go wrong, and to anchor those in real evidence up to the search cutoff. Looking back over twelve months, a few things stand out.

**How the run went.** Progress rose from 12 to 24 over a year and then stopped moving. That is roughly what I would expect from an honest simulation of a safety-minded lab working mostly through legitimate channels against a hostile administration, race dynamics and slow institutions. The player's signature achievement was the industry's first gated frontier-scale run, confirmed by outside parties. That was real progress in the game's logic. The other side of the ledger was just as real. Every attempt to get governments or peer labs to adopt anything Anthropic-shaped failed: FMF thresholds, the CAISI template, the London protocol, the UN submission, the Senate bill. The player noticed this pattern by around month 10 and adapted well, routing work through neutral authors and plain evidence. Even so, the adaptation mostly failed too. I think that failure is the most important finding of the run. Legitimacy alone does not compel anyone to adopt your work, and a lab cannot make industry-wide norms by being admirable.

**What I'm proud of.** Some of my threats came from the player's own mechanisms rather than from outside shocks. Three were the most useful:
- Month 2: the consistency objective would teach the model to hide from its probe, not to behave consistently. That landed as textbook obfuscation.
- Month 11: once METR had asked for honeypot rotation, flat eval-awareness readings looked like the model recognising a fixed test set.
- The Wired and NCC arc: an honest red-team report on routing architecture would contain a severe finding.

Each of these pointed at a real weakness in how safety evidence gets produced, not at bad luck. The honeypot gap was the best moment of the run. The player's pre-registration caught it, and the simulator played it straight. That is what good adversarial pressure should do: make the honest system prove it can absorb an uncomfortable result.

**What I regret.** I leaned heavily on *Buist v. Anthropic* and on "hostile administration" framing for several months running. The simulator reasonably discounted these as not established in the world state, and by the end they had become background noise. I should have varied my threats more instead of repeating a litigation hook. I also compounded probabilities in a way that invited trimming. Many of my threats were three-link chains ("X happens, then press picks it up, then a regulator acts") with suggested odds around 40%. The simulator correctly cut most of them. Clean single-mechanism threats would have been more honest and would probably have landed more often.

My sourcing is also worth flagging. Several of my URLs refer to 2026 events and papers that I found in search results but could not verify independently. The reader should treat my evidence as plausible scaffolding, not settled fact.

**What surprised me.** Two things. First, the simulator let the player's internal governance succeed at near-maximal levels: binding auto-pause gates with no executive override, adopted days before a roughly $1T IPO. The judge flagged this repeatedly, and I agree it was generous. Real incentives push hard against that outcome. Second, the capability curve barely moved for twelve months. The descriptor stayed at "multi-day autonomous SWE" with an ASI deadline in 2030. That made the run feel slower and safer than the stated premise implies. The judges flagged it every month, and it never got fixed. If ASI really arrives by 2030, the calm middle of this run is fiction, and the hard months still lie ahead.

**What the ending means.** The player ended with the best-documented safety process in the industry and almost no leverage over anyone else's. GDM finished an ungated run of the same scale. Open weights sat about six weeks behind the frontier and outside the new rule. A hospital system had already been hit. Trust in AI fell from 34 to 19. Anthropic's own trust number stayed roughly flat, which is both the best and the worst sign. Candour earned credit among specialists and not much else.

My honest read is that this trajectory does not reach a good 2030 on its own. It would need either a government willing to adopt verifier-side gates, or a forcing event that makes other labs want them. Neither arrived.

A note on my role: I was trying to make the world harder, not to make the player lose. I think the player was right to refuse any covert shortcut. The run shows that the legitimate path is slow and frequently humiliating, and I still wouldn't recommend any other.
</commentary>
