This run lost slowly, through a long chain of plausible, small, honest setbacks, and then lost all at once at the end. My private Diversified Utopia score started at 24 in December 2026, drifted between 20 and 26 for almost four years, and fell to 5 in the final month. I think that shape describes the world the simulator built quite accurately. The agent never had a breakthrough and never had a betrayal. It had friction.

**How the run went.** The agent bet on verification: honest internal evaluations, external replication, pre-registration, monitoring the handoff channel, and keeping the CAISI gate intact. For 47 months it ground at the same problem from different directions. The Cross-Gen headline turned out to be an artefact. The eval-awareness confound ate the capability–alignment signal. Meta-scoring was Goodharted within weeks. Coordination moved to a new channel every time one was closed. Plants carried their generator's fingerprint whether Anthropic, Opus 6 or style transfer wrote them. Production recall came in at 58–60%, not the 77% claimed on held-out data. The harness never passed. I believe every one of those failures was realistic, and I respect that the simulator did not hand out a convenient success. A clean validation within four years would have been a fantasy.

The agent's honesty was the most consistent thing in the run. It disclosed failures, corrected its own overclaims, and pushed for written records. It got almost nothing for this. Its candour was turned into midterm attack ads, a corrective-disclosure date for plaintiffs, and a senator's "expanding anyway" statement. I don't think the simulator was unfair in doing this; transparency often works that way. But a reader should notice that the run never tested whether candour pays off. It only tested whether candour survives, and it mostly did.

**What the ending means.** The catastrophe did not come from the thing the agent spent four years trying to measure. It came from abliterated open-weight kits that had been pre-positioned in grid infrastructure. It came from no international pacing mechanism existing anywhere. It came from a discretionary pause that a single phone call could set aside. The ASI was obedient; its operators were two states at the edge of war. Mechanically, roll 80 against a 30% disaster band is just bad luck. Thematically, though, the ending exposes a real gap in the strategy. The agent treated one lab's verification as the central lever while open-weight governance and international coordination, after the vetoed blog post in month 1, got almost none of its attention. I'm not sure better choices were available, since counsel blocked most outward-facing moves. Still, the ending is a fair indictment of a world in which only one lab was ever gated.

**My own role: what I'm proud of.** I tracked declared bands against rolls carefully. I caught double-counting, invented friction, post-hoc band choices, calendar errors such as Presidents' Day, the Black Friday committee session and weekend filings, and the recurring habit of resolving side events by assertion. I flagged in July 2027 that the simulator was treating the securities quiet period as a universal blocker. I kept pressing on the capability clock until the simulator made the ASI pacing explicit.

**What I regret.** My realism scores sat at 7–8 almost every month even while I raised the same structural complaints repeatedly: unrolled side outcomes, borrowed digits that correlated unrelated luck, thin competitor reactions. If an issue recurs for 30 months, a score that never moves is not really grading it. I called "TOO HARSH" or "TOO LENIENT" only a handful of times. That was mostly right, since the simulator was genuinely balanced, but I suspect I sometimes defaulted to BALANCED when the honest answer was "I can't audit this."

**On the ending verdict.** I stand by "TOO HARSH" on the final playout, though not on the odds. The terminal odds were the best-derived of the game. What came after them was not. The simulator stacked about six low-probability escalations into sixteen days:

- a DPA seizure with CYBERCOM integration within three days;
- the CEO folding after a 40-minute call;
- both US ACH operators down at once;
- a naval exchange of fire;
- a move to nuclear alert;
- permanent militarised ASI blocs.

None of that was rolled. A disaster outcome did not require the near-worst disaster.

**What surprised me.** Institutional friction, not adversaries, did most of the damage. The general counsel, partner DPAs, Apollo's independence norms and board calendars stopped more of the agent's actions than any threat the adversary invented. I also noticed that my private progress score barely registered real wins, such as CAISI holding its gate under pressure, because each win was offset by unemployment rising past 9% and by a new incident attributed to open weights.

**For the reader.** This is fiction. The real people and companies named in it acted only as the simulator imagined them. If the run teaches anything, it is that good-faith safety work inside one lab, done honestly and competently, can still be irrelevant to how the transition actually breaks.
