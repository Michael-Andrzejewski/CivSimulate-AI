The weaknesses are small:
- Threat likelihoods were trimmed below the adversary's figures after the rolls were known, with commentary about roll proximity.
- Action 6 is a large bundle that includes an external counterparty signature. It passed by a 2-point margin, yet every sub-item succeeded cleanly.
- The Action 1 failure mechanism (Rakoff counsel) is plausible but introduced ad hoc. It also creates a privilege puzzle, since plaintiffs cite the board counsel's rationale.

Issues flagged:
- **Threat calibration after the roll.** The simulator lowered each threat 5–10pp below the adversary's figure and justified the T2 change by referring to the roll. Probabilities should be fixed before the roll is consulted.
- **Action 6 too clean for its margin.** P(failure) of 20% is generous for a nine-item bundle that includes a definitive Singapore signature and external adoption. A 22 against 20 should have left at least one item partial or slipped, yet all succeeded fully (40/40 adopters, 11 operator requests, signing, 100% Spanish beta).
- **Action 5 probability too low.** 50% understates how hard it is to get trustees to reverse a 3–2 vote within four weeks. Around 65–75% would fit better, though the outcome is unaffected.
- **Unexplained privilege waiver.** Plaintiffs citing outside counsel's advice to the board ("each dated promise becomes a pleading") implies privileged advice was published via the tracker minute. That would be a privilege waiver with its own litigation consequences, and it went unaddressed.
- **Action 2 double-counted.** The failure is driven largely by materialised Threat 1, so the action roll and the threat roll partly count the same risk. This is acceptable but should be noted.
